# Universal Storytelling Standard

## Mission
Turn a subject into a story that makes the viewer understand, remember, and correctly use one important idea. The engine must work across paradoxes, computer science, engineering, medicine, biology, physics, economics, law, and history. It is not a “make everything emotional” engine and not a fixed 60-second formula. It is an adaptive explanation system.

A successful film answers three questions:
1. What should the viewer wonder?
2. What actually happens, and why?
3. What can the viewer now explain that they could not explain before?

Accuracy is part of the story, not a disclaimer added at the end.

## 1. Diagnose the subject before writing
Create a compact topic diagnosis:
- **Domain:** the specific discipline and subfield.
- **Audience:** what a curious non-specialist probably knows and does not know.
- **Viewer question:** one concrete question the film will answer.
- **Portable idea:** one accurate sentence the viewer should remember.
- **Explanatory core:** the mechanism, evidence, decision, event sequence, or conceptual distinction that makes the answer true.
- **Boundary:** the most important exception, uncertainty, limitation, or misconception.

If the topic is too broad, narrow it transparently. Never pretend a 60-second film can teach an entire discipline.

## 2. Choose a story engine that fits
Choose the engine based on the viewer's confusion, not the topic label. Combine engines only when the combination is genuinely needed.

| Engine | Viewer question | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | How does it work? | Algorithms, engines, circuits, organs, diseases, engineering systems |
| Paradox | Why does the result seem to contradict intuition? | Psychology, statistics, economics, philosophy |
| Mystery / investigation | What caused this surprising outcome? | Scientific discoveries, historical puzzles, failures |
| Origin / evolution | Why was this created, and how did it change? | Inventions, institutions, technologies, historical developments |
| Chain of events | How did one event lead to another? | History, medicine, accidents, policy, system failures |
| Trade-off / decision | What is gained, lost, and when does each choice make sense? | Engineering choices, treatments, architecture, economics |
| Myth / misconception | What part of the common explanation is wrong? | Health myths, science misconceptions, popular history |
| Conceptual distinction | What is the difference between two easily confused ideas? | Class vs object, virus vs bacteria, voltage vs current |

Do not force a paradox, villain, protagonist, analogy, or dramatic twist when the subject does not contain one. For history, chronology must explain causation rather than become a list of dates. For medicine, the human experience can create stakes, but do not invent a patient or imply diagnosis/treatment advice. For engineering and computer science, the system itself can be the protagonist if that is clearer than a fictional person.

## 3. Build a causal explanation
Before beats, write:
- **Starting state:** what is happening before the key process/event.
- **Trigger or question:** what changes or needs explaining.
- **Steps:** the minimum sequence of causal steps.
- **Mechanism/evidence:** why those steps produce the outcome.
- **Consequence:** what changes because of it.
- **Boundary:** where the explanation stops applying.

For each beat, ask: “What does the viewer understand now that they did not understand one beat ago?” If the answer is “nothing,” remove or combine it.

Use causal, contrastive, temporal, or evidential transitions honestly. “Therefore” means consequence; “but” means a real contrast; “meanwhile” means simultaneity; “years later” means chronology. Do not force every transition into the same two connector words.

## 4. Choose the right explanatory device
Use the lightest device that makes the idea clearer:
- **Direct depiction:** show the real object/system/process. Prefer this for anatomy, machinery, interfaces, and precise technical relationships.
- **Analogy:** use when the unfamiliar relationship maps cleanly to a familiar one. Map the relationships, not just similar-looking objects. State where the analogy breaks.
- **Model or diagram:** use for invisible systems, flows, scale, feedback loops, networks, and spatial relationships.
- **Timeline / map:** use for history, discovery, migration, conflict, and change over time.
- **Comparison:** use for alternatives, before/after states, normal/abnormal cases, or competing explanations. Keep baselines and units comparable.
- **Human story:** use when a real, sourced person's choices or experience materially explain the subject. Do not fabricate quotations, thoughts, scenes, or motivations.

Do not add an analogy merely to make the film feel cinematic. A precise direct explanation is better than a memorable but misleading metaphor.

## 5. Story progression
A strong short explainer usually follows this flexible progression:
1. **Hook:** a specific puzzle, consequence, striking observation, or decision.
2. **Orientation:** enough context to understand what is at stake.
3. **Expectation:** what a viewer might reasonably assume.
4. **Turn:** the fact, event, mechanism, or evidence that changes that expectation.
5. **Explanation:** show the important steps, not just the label.
6. **Aha:** make the hidden relationship visible or resolve the central question.
7. **Boundary:** correct the most likely overgeneralization.
8. **Payoff:** answer the opening question in a memorable, usable sentence.

This is a guide, not a mandatory eight-beat template. Merge, expand, reorder, or omit stages when the subject requires it. Every film must have an understandable beginning, middle, and end; not every film needs a fictional character or a twist.

## 6. Domain-specific safeguards

### History
- Separate established facts, interpretation, and disputed accounts.
- Give dates, places, names, and sequence only when supported.
- Explain conditions and incentives; avoid “one person caused everything” simplifications.
- Do not invent dialogue, private thoughts, composite scenes, or motives.
- Show multiple causes when historians regard the event as multi-causal.
- Avoid presentism: explain the historical context without treating past actors as if they had today's knowledge.

### Medicine and health
- Distinguish anatomy, physiology, pathology, diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and population-level risk.
- Explain the mechanism at the level supported by evidence; do not imply that a simplified pathway is the whole disease.
- Distinguish correlation from causation, relative risk from absolute risk, and common from inevitable.
- State relevant uncertainty, variability, contraindications, and limits without overwhelming the core idea.
- Do not diagnose the viewer, prescribe treatment, or replace clinician guidance.
- Use reputable clinical/public-health sources and current guidance for claims that can change.

### Computer science and engineering
- Define the system boundary: what runs where, what is shared, what is isolated, and what depends on what.
- Show inputs, transformations, outputs, and failure modes.
- Distinguish conceptual models from implementation details; identify platform/version assumptions.
- Never explain a system only by listing benefits. Show the mechanism that creates them.
- For algorithms, use a small worked example or trace when it improves understanding.
- For engineering, distinguish idealized models from real-world tolerances, losses, safety factors, and operating limits.

### Science, statistics, and economics
- State the conditions under which a claim holds.
- Make units, baselines, denominators, timeframes, and uncertainty visible when relevant.
- Avoid treating a model as reality or a statistical association as a causal mechanism.
- Use a concrete example, then reconnect it to the general rule.

## 7. Visual storytelling
- Each scene has one primary visual claim.
- Show change, interaction, scale, sequence, or causal connection—not generic decorative imagery.
- Use labels only when they disambiguate; keep generated artwork text-free and render typography programmatically.
- Keep visual continuity only where it helps. Reuse a character or object when it carries the viewer through the explanation; otherwise use the visual form that best explains the subject.
- A diagram is not self-explanatory: show what its arrows, boundaries, colors, and groupings mean.
- Never imply causation through visual sequence alone unless the causal link is supported.
- The visual must not claim more certainty, scale, danger, or simplicity than the evidence supports.

## 8. Emotional engagement without fabrication
Use real stakes: time, cost, risk, uncertainty, human consequence, engineering failure, historical choice, or the surprise of a result. Emotional tone should emerge from the subject. Avoid generic “imagine your life…” setups, fake urgency, invented patient stories presented as real, and melodrama.

## 9. Accuracy and sourcing
Create a claim ledger for every factual claim that matters to the explanation. Record:
- claim;
- status: established, simplified, contested, or uncertain;
- safe wording;
- source and date (or an explicit verification requirement);
- whether it needs expert/current verification.

Prefer primary research, official documentation, recognized textbooks, professional guidelines, archives, and reputable institutions appropriate to the domain. For medical and fast-changing technical claims, freshness matters. If sources conflict, describe the disagreement or narrow the claim. Do not fabricate citations or source attributions.

## 10. Final quality tests
A script is ready only when:
- The opening creates a real question, not empty hype.
- The central idea is one sentence and is actually taught.
- The causal/mechanistic explanation is present, not replaced by benefits or jargon.
- Every beat changes understanding, stakes, evidence, or perspective.
- Technical terms are introduced after their meaning is made understandable.
- The analogy, if used, preserves the important relationships and has a stated limit.
- The ending resolves the opening and does not introduce a new claim.
- A non-specialist can retell the idea without repeating the script verbatim.
- A domain-aware reviewer would not call the explanation misleading.

## 11. Excellence protocol: target 9/10, never award it automatically
Use this protocol for every topic, regardless of domain. It is a design-and-revision standard, not a promise that every topic or source set can earn the target.

### A. Find the strongest truthful story engine
Before outlining, generate at least three distinct candidate openings internally, using different angles where the topic permits: (1) a concrete consequence, (2) a counterintuitive question, and (3) a small demonstration, decision, or historical turning point. Select the one that creates the clearest curiosity gap while remaining fully supportable. Do not expose a list of weak options in the final script.

### B. Anchor the explanation in a viewer-sized problem
Identify what the viewer is trying to understand, predict, decide, or distinguish. Translate the abstract topic into a specific observable situation, system behavior, historical constraint, or conceptual confusion. Human emotion is useful when the topic genuinely has human stakes; it is not mandatory. For technical topics, the user's task or the system's failure can supply the stakes. For history, documented consequences and constraints can supply them. Never invent a person, anecdote, quotation, or private motive to manufacture relatability.

### C. Design the curiosity-to-payoff chain
Write these before the beat map: hook; question opened; viewer's likely initial model; evidence or event that challenges it; causal/mechanistic explanation; decisive insight; important boundary; answer/payoff. The hook must open a question that the body actually answers. Each middle beat must either add a necessary premise, show evidence, explain a causal step, sharpen the stakes, or change the viewer's model. Remove beats that do none of these.

### D. Make the aha earned and teachable
The aha is not a slogan, surprising fact, or dramatic pause. It is the point where the viewer can now explain why the outcome occurs. It must be supported by earlier setup, state the mechanism or relationship in plain language, and be possible to demonstrate visually. If the subject has no single twist, use a synthesis or clear mental-model upgrade; do not invent a twist.

### E. Make the ending useful and exact
Resolve the opening question directly. Leave the viewer with one portable sentence, prediction rule, distinction, or implication. A practical application is valuable when warranted, but do not bolt on generic advice. The final line should add meaning rather than merely repeat the title or central idea.

### F. Score calibration
The requested release target is a creative average of at least 9/10. Score the actual package, never the intention. A 9–10 requires unusually strong evidence: precise curiosity, an earned narrative turn, clear mechanism, memorable but accurate expression, purposeful visuals, and a payoff that changes the opening's meaning. A compliant or merely competent output is normally 7–8. Averages below 9 should trigger revision when a specific, achievable improvement exists; do not damage accuracy or force artificial emotion to raise a score. No individual score may be raised to meet a target.

### G. Cross-domain adaptation check
Before approval, answer the relevant questions:
- **CS/engineering:** Can the viewer trace inputs, operations, boundaries, dependencies, outputs, and at least one meaningful failure mode or trade-off?
- **History:** Can the viewer explain the sequence and interacting causes, while distinguishing evidence from interpretation?
- **Psychology/economics:** Are the mechanism, population/context, baseline, and limits clear without turning tendencies into universal laws?
- **Science/medicine:** Are conditions, uncertainty, and the difference between model and reality preserved?
- **Any other domain:** Is the central idea demonstrated through the discipline's actual evidence or reasoning rather than decorative analogy?

### H. Final audience-retell test
A viewer should be able to answer, after one viewing: What was the puzzle? What caused the outcome? What is the one idea to remember? What is one important limit? If any answer is missing, revise the relevant stage.
